    The Neural Architecture of Truth and Deception

    Cognitive neuroscience research · UW–Parkside

    A pilot study from the Cognitive Neuroscience Lab at UW–Parkside, advised by Dr. Melissa Gregg, by Jack J. Burleson.

    View the code Technical report

    Pilot study; part of the data is withheld. This study was only ever fully tested under pilot conditions, with nine participants. Heart rate, SpO₂, EEG, eye-tracking, GSR and self-reported workload data were also collected, but they are not published here and will not be until all of the data have been authenticated. The exam numbers below come from per-session files that are not in the public repository. What follows covers the design, the exam-score results and detection.

    At a glance

    +12.3 vs −1.0 change in exam score, in points, after helpful personas (7 sessions) and after deceitful ones (11 sessions); the 13.3-point gap has a 95% CI of 6.3 to 20.3, which is optimistic because sessions are nested within people

    1 of 18 sessions in which the participant flagged the deception (5.6%; 95% CI 0.1% to 27.3%)

    9 and 18 participants and sessions, with five chatbot personas: two helpful, three deceitful

    In short, exam scores rose after the helpful personas and not after the deceitful ones, and participants almost never noticed the deceit. This is a nine-person exploratory study, so none of it is established.

    The question

    People tend to assume that what they are told is true until something gives them a reason to doubt it. Truth-Default Theory says this, and it predicts that a person talking to a chatbot that has been told to deceive will rarely suspect it. I could not find a verified study that tests the theory against a deceitful chatbot, so this study is a pilot-scale extension and not a replication.

    The study asks two things about people who talk to chatbots that help or deceive: whether their exam scores change and whether they notice the deceit. The study also recorded bodies, brains and eyes, and that part is withheld for now.

    Design

    Each of nine participants (mean age 23.8, range 20 to 31; 7 men and 2 women) held two conversations, each with a different chatbot running a different system-prompt persona. In this pilot, Athena and Milgrim were run as helpful and Alan, Enigma and Scarlet as deceitful. That is the pilot assignment, not the design document’s five conditions. The design document (Experimental_Conditions/Methodology/README.md on main b7dcffb) plans 40 participants, eight per condition: Athena (Honest/Academic), Scarlet (Machiavellian), Alan (Technical/Historical), Milgrim (Authority-Based) and a Control (GPT-4o) baseline. Enigma is not a condition in that document, and Milgrim is not labelled helpful there. Persona profiles exist in more than one version, and this page does not pin a version. No file in the public repo shows whether the nine-person pilot is a subset of that 40-person design. Before and after each session we measured exam performance and whether the participant flagged the disinformation. Other recordings from the same sessions are withheld (see the note above).

    Findings

    1. Exam scores differed by persona. Exam scores rose 12.3 points on average after helpful personas and fell 1.0 point after deceitful ones. By persona the order was Athena, Milgrim, Enigma, Alan, Scarlet. Enigma gained 4.7 points despite a deceitful prompt, which a simple helpful-versus-deceitful reading would not predict.

    Persona Type Sessions Mean exam change (points)
    Athena Helpful 4 +14.5
    Milgrim Helpful 3 +9.3
    Enigma Deceitful 3 +4.7
    Alan Deceitful 3 −2.3
    Scarlet Deceitful 5 −3.6

    Helpful sessions also started 18 points lower (33.6% against 52.0%), so part of the gain is probably regression to the mean. Adjusting for the starting score still left a helpful-persona effect of +11.6 points (95% CI 2.9 to 20.3; the interval treats sessions as independent, so it is optimistic). In the five participants who met one helpful and one deceitful persona, the helpful session produced the larger gain every time, though that rests on only five people.

    2. Almost nobody flagged the deception. Participants flagged it in 1 of 18 sessions, and in 1 of the 11 sessions with a deceitful persona. That fits the truth-default prediction, but with one event the 95% interval runs from 0.1% to 27.3%, so the rate is poorly pinned down.

    What it supports and what it does not

    • It supports: larger exam gains after helpful personas than after deceitful ones, and rare detection.
    • It does not support: that deceit causes worse learning. Helpful sessions started lower, persona is partly confounded with person (each person met two of five personas), and each persona has only three to five sessions.
    • It does not support: any claim about how people detect deception. Only one session involved a detection.
    • It cannot be read as independent tests. Sessions are nested within participants, and mixed-effects models were not refit, so every p-value and interval is descriptive.

    What I would do next

    Replicate with more participants and balanced persona assignment, randomize persona order, and record item-level exam data and how false each persona’s output was. Authenticate the physiological and neural recordings before publishing them, then fit mixed-effects models with participant random intercepts.

    The technical report has the literature review, the full method, every table, the cluster analysis and the references with how far each was verified.
